Water loss in a business property, and why it differs from a house

Commercial water damage means water in a building that is not someone's home: a leased office suite, a retail unit in a strip center, or a building with several occupants sharing walls, ceilings and plumbing. The wet drywall and subfloor are the same. What changes is who owns the space and who holds the keys.

Little Elm's Census population grew from 25,898 in 2010 to 46,453 in 2020. More homes bring more shops and offices, and many of those buildings are newer, with ceilings and shared walls that carry water from one unit to the next. If you manage or lease space here, find out now which shut-off valve serves your unit.

The ceiling tile is stained and the carpet squishes

A brown ring on a ceiling tile, carpet that squishes near the break room, or a musty smell when you unlock in the morning all point to water that has been moving for a while. The trade term for finding where it went and getting it out is water mitigation, covered in more detail on our water mitigation page. For a business, the first job is a clear picture of how far it spread.

Electrical, access and occupant safety

Keep employees, customers and visitors out of wet areas until the source is stopped and the floor is no longer a slip hazard.

Water near an electrical panel, outlets, a server closet or lighting in a wet ceiling means nobody goes in. Report any shock risk, sparks or a gas smell to the utility or 911 before you call us. For sprinkler or fire system water, contact the property manager at once.

Tell us early about after-hours access and who holds keys. A locked suite can cost more time than the water does.

Who can authorize the work: owner, manager, tenant or landlord

Before crews start, someone with authority has to say yes. That may be the owner, a property manager, the tenant who leases the unit, or the landlord, depending on the lease and on what part of the building is wet. We can start extraction in an area when the person who controls the space approves it.

Who pays, and whether the tenant or the landlord is responsible for a given repair, is a matter between those parties and their lease. We do not give legal advice on that. Read the lease and talk to your attorney, and tell us whom to send records to so everyone has the same file.

How we work a commercial water loss

Dispatch is 24/7, and we work to schedule the same day where we can. A technician walks the space with you or the manager, finds the source, and checks the affected rooms with moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera. Ceiling cavities and walls shared with the next unit get the same attention as the visible floor.

Extraction units pull standing water from hard floors, carpet tile and low spots. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers then dry what stays, and readings are taken again on each visit. The drying science is explained on our structural drying page, and we ask any company you consider whether its crews work to ANSI/IICRC S500, the published water damage standard.

Access and sequencing are worked out with the property manager or owner, so unaffected rooms can stay reachable while wet areas are dried in stages.

Drywall, ceiling tile and carpet tile: what stays and what goes

MaterialWhat we look forUsual decision
Ceiling tileSagging, staining, soft edgesWet tile is usually replaced, since it rarely dries flat
DrywallMoisture reading above the wicking line, soft paper faceDry in place if caught early, cut out if saturated or contaminated
Carpet tileWet backing, wet adhesive, water under the tilesLift tiles to dry the slab, replace what stays wet or smells
Insulation in wallsMatted or heavy battsUsually removed and replaced after the cavity dries
Hard floors and slabMoisture trapped under finishesExtract and dry, then recheck with the meter

The meter decides, not guesswork. Rebuilding afterward, such as new ceiling tile or paint, is a separate scope for a contractor the owner or manager chooses.

Records for the owner, the manager and the insurer

We document what we find. That means photos of the damage before work begins, moisture readings logged on each visit, and a written scope of what was removed and dried. The owner or manager gets a copy to pass to the insurer and the other party.

Texas Department of Insurance guidance is general but sound: stop the water, remove standing water, photograph the damage and keep receipts. Keep removed items until the adjuster has seen them.

Common mistakes that make a business loss worse

  • Waiting for the next business day to find someone who can authorize work.
  • Leaving wet carpet tile or ceiling tile in place because it looks fine.
  • Closing walls before readings confirm the cavity is dry.
  • Throwing away damaged stock before photographs and receipts are saved.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you come to a business after hours or on a weekend?

Our dispatch line runs 24/7, so a call at night or on a Sunday is answered. Same-day help depends on the crew schedule, and we say so up front. Have the address, suite number and the name of the person who can authorize work ready when you call.

Who has to approve the work, the tenant or the landlord?

That depends on the lease and on which part of the building is wet. We can work with the owner, the property manager, the tenant or the landlord, and we need one clear approval before starting. Who ends up paying is for those parties and their attorney, because we do not give legal advice.

Do you provide a record for the insurance adjuster?

Yes. We keep photos, moisture readings and a written scope, and give the owner or manager a copy. Commercial policies vary, so ask your agent or broker what they require.

A neighboring unit leaked into mine. What do I do first?

Stop any source you control, photograph the damage, and tell your property manager and the neighboring occupant. Then call us so the affected walls and ceiling can be checked with a meter. Responsibility between the units is a lease question.
