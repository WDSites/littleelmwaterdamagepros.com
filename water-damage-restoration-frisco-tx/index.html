How Frisco's rapid growth shaped what fails in its homes

Frisco counted 33,714 residents in 2000, then 116,989 in 2010, then 200,509 in 2020. That is almost sixfold growth in two decades, and most of the housing arrived in waves, one subdivision after another. Homes finished in the same few seasons age together, so their water heaters, washer hoses and ice-maker tubing tend to wear out in the same few years.

That clustering has a simple payoff for you. If your house came out of the 2000s or 2010s building boom, find the manufacture date on the water heater label and look at the hoses behind the washer. Replace anything bulging, rusted or impossible to date before a Saturday morning flood makes the choice for you.

Roofs and attics follow the same calendar. Shingles from those waves have spent years under north Texas hail and summer heat, and one lifted tab can let rain into the attic long before a ceiling stain appears. After a hard storm, check the attic with a flashlight for dark decking or damp insulation.

The city itself began in 1902 as a railroad watering stop, so water has been Frisco's business from the start. Your version of that job is smaller. Know where your main shutoff valve sits, confirm it turns by hand, and make sure every adult in the house can find it in the dark.

When water is spreading in Frisco

A wet floor, a dripping ceiling or a gurgling drain in Frisco is an emergency. Emergency dispatch available around the clock means a 2 a.m. call is handled like a noon call, and a crew leaves the Little Elm base as soon as one is free to go, same day when available. Clean supply-line water can still ruin drywall, baseboards and subfloor if it sits. Sewage or rising water is a different problem, classed as Category 3 under ANSI/IICRC S500. In a two-story Frisco house, thermal cameras and moisture meters find the wet drywall behind a ceiling stain before extraction units and air movers go to work. If water has reached an outlet, a panel or a ceiling fixture, call the electric utility or 911 before you call us.

Before We Arrive: your first steps

Stop the source

Close the fixture valve or the main shutoff. If you cannot find the leak, the main is the safe default.

Cut power only if safe

Switch off breakers for wet areas only if you can reach the panel while standing on a dry floor.

Lift what you can

Move rugs, electronics, books and small furniture onto dry surfaces. Leave heavy pieces for the crew.

Keep people and pets out

Stay clear of sewage or any water that touched outlets, and keep children and animals off wet floors.

Photograph everything

Take wide and close photos or video before you move items, and keep damaged property for the adjuster.

Two counties, one city water system

Frisco sits mostly in Collin County with a part in Denton County. Before any rebuild that touches plumbing, framing or electrical, ask which city or county office handles permits for your address, because the answer depends on which side of the county line you live on.

The City of Frisco buys treated water from the North Texas Municipal Water District and keeps an on-call Public Works employee available 24/7. Water Operations handles water lines, valves and hydrants. A broken main or a hydrant gushing in the street is the city's call to take. Water inside your walls, after your meter, is yours to shut off, and ours to dry.

Frisco ISD serves most of the city, with parts in Lewisville, Little Elm and Prosper ISDs. Give dispatch your street address rather than a school zone, since district lines do not follow the city map.

Records that help a Frisco insurance claim

A burst pipe or a split washer hose is the sort of sudden loss many policies pay for, while a slow drip often is not. Tell your insurer quickly, and do it as soon as you spot hidden damage, since waiting can hurt a claim.

Film the damage, save receipts for emergency steps and wait for the adjuster before making anything permanent. A tarp or a shutoff is fine, a new subfloor is not.
